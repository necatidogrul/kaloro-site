Best AI calorie counter apps for iPhone (2026)

By Necati Dogrul · Updated 12 September 2026 · US App Store rating counts read via the public iTunes lookup API on 12 September 2026

Disclosure: I develop Kaloro, which is number 10 on this list. It is placed last because it has the fewest ratings. The list is ordered by rating count, not by my opinion.

The one thing most lists get wrong

The model is not the problem. Every app here uses a capable vision model, and on a restaurant plate or a packaged snack they all land close enough. Where they all fail is home cooking, and they fail the same way: the photo cannot see the oil in the pan, the butter stirred in at the end, or whether the plate is small or large. That is worth 20–50% of the calories in a home-cooked meal, and it is invisible to every camera.

So the question to ask of an AI calorie counter is not "how accurate is the AI" but "what happens when the photo is wrong?" Three answers exist:

  1. The app gives you a bare number and you trust it. (Most apps.)
  2. The app lets you edit the ingredient list after the fact. (Cal AI, Lose It, MyFitnessPal.)
  3. The app tells you what it assumed — oil, portion — and lets you fix those two things in a tap. (Kaloro, and to a degree SnapCalorie's volume estimate.)

How to choose

Packaged food, barcodes, restaurant chains: MyFitnessPal or Lose It. The database matters more than the camera.

Photo-first and you want the most popular option: Cal AI. Be aware that the onboarding is a long questionnaire that ends at a paywall; that is the most common complaint in its recent one-star reviews.

Europe or Turkey, mixed packaged and fresh food: Yazio, which has the best regional database. Its Turkish-language support is thinner than its database.

You cook at home and the numbers keep coming in low: Kaloro, or any app that lets you correct oil and portion rather than just the ingredient list. This is the case I built Kaloro for, so weigh that accordingly.

What none of them can do

No photo can measure the oil you cooked with. Any app that claims a single accuracy percentage for "food photos" is quoting a restaurant-plate benchmark. On home cooking the honest answer is a range, and the app should show you which end of the range it picked.
