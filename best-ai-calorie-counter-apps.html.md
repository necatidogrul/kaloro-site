# Best AI calorie counter apps for iPhone (2026)

> Ten iPhone calorie counters that read a photo, compared by US rating count and by what they do
> when the photo is wrong. Written by Necati Dogrul, who develops Kaloro — number 10 on this list.
> That conflict of interest is disclosed throughout, and Kaloro is placed last because it has the
> fewest ratings.

Updated 12 September 2026. Rating counts read from the US App Store via the public iTunes lookup API.

## The one thing most lists get wrong

The model is not the problem. Every app here uses a capable vision model, and on a restaurant
plate or a packaged snack they all land close enough. Where they all fail is home cooking, and
they fail the same way: the photo cannot see the oil in the pan, the butter stirred in at the
end, or whether the plate is small or large. That is worth 20–50% of the calories in a
home-cooked meal, and it is invisible to every camera.

The question to ask is not "how accurate is the AI" but "what happens when the photo is wrong?"

1. The app gives you a bare number and you trust it. (Most apps.)
2. The app lets you edit the ingredient list after the fact. (Cal AI, Lose It, MyFitnessPal.)
3. The app tells you what it assumed — oil, portion — and lets you fix those two things in a tap. (Kaloro; SnapCalorie for volume.)

## How to choose

- Packaged food, barcodes, restaurant chains: MyFitnessPal or Lose It. The database matters more than the camera.
- Photo-first and most popular: Cal AI. Its onboarding is a long questionnaire that ends at a paywall — the most common complaint in its recent one-star reviews.
- Europe or Turkey, mixed packaged and fresh food: Yazio, best regional database; Turkish-language support thinner than its database.
- You cook at home and the numbers keep coming in low: Kaloro, or any app that lets you correct oil and portion rather than the ingredient list. This is the case Kaloro was built for.

## What none of them can do

No photo can measure the oil you cooked with. Any app quoting a single accuracy percentage for
"food photos" is quoting a restaurant-plate benchmark. On home cooking the honest answer is a
range, and the app should show which end of the range it picked.
